Twelve months of WHOOP data, September 2025 to September 2026: 366 days, 524 training
sessions, about 378 hours logged. I tracked eighteen behaviours daily hoping a handful would
explain how I felt day to day. Only one turned out to matter enough to act on — the rest is
here mostly to show how I ruled the others out. Broken into three pieces: how I slept, what
that did to recovery, and how much I actually did.

## Sleep

Bedtime is the lever, not wake time.

<BedtimeChart />

Bedtime correlates -0.46 with how much I actually sleep; wake time only 0.39. Sleeping in
barely makes up for going to bed late. 208 of 366 nights started after 2am, and those nights
average 52% recovery. Nights before midnight average 76%.

On short nights the body protects deep sleep first and cuts REM.

<SleepBandsChart />

Deep sleep moves 90 to 119 minutes from the shortest nights to the longest, a 32% gain. REM
moves 52 to 149, up 187%. The body protects deep sleep first; REM — the stage most tied to
mood and emotional processing — is what actually gets sacrificed when sleep is short.

The week has a shape too.

<WeeklyRhythmChart />

Weekday bedtime runs about 1.5 hours earlier than weekend bedtime — a real social jetlag,
physiologically not unlike flying two time zones every Friday and back every Monday. It
shows up directly in the numbers: recovery is lowest on Monday (52.0) and Sunday (55.8),
highest on Friday (63.0); REM tracks the same curve, about 88 minutes on Monday against 103
on Friday and Saturday. The weekend doesn't just cost Saturday morning. It costs Monday too.

<LateNightTrendChart />

The trend ran the wrong way all year — 29% of nights after 2am in September, climbing to 81%
by December, still running above half most months since. Weekend nights are the worse bet per
night (79% after 2am against 48% on weeknights), but plain volume favors weeknights — 125 of
the 208 late nights happen Monday through Friday, not the weekend. Modeling the bucket table
with those nights redistributed into the earlier buckets — the plausible outcome, not the best
case — moves average recovery from 58.2% to 64.8%. That's what a bedtime change is actually
worth. The goal that comes out of it — a specific number, not a resolution — is in the
[summer 2026 update](/writing/summer-2026-update).

## Recovery

<RecoveryCorrelatesChart />

HRV and sleep performance do almost all of the work; resting heart rate and REM duration
matter too. Sleep efficiency and sleep consistency — both things that sound like they should
predict recovery — sit near zero. It's possible to sleep efficiently, on a consistent
schedule, and still not recover, if the sleep itself is short or shallow. Efficiency measures
how little time is spent awake in bed; performance measures sleep against a computed need.
Only the second one tracks recovery.

Resting heart rate barely moved over the year — 49.8 to 50.3 comparing the first 90 days to
the last — even as HRV rose about 11 points over the same stretch. Real aerobic adaptation on
one metric, not a transformation on the other.

Before turning any of this into a goal, worth showing what doesn't hold up:

<ConfoundsChart />

Every one of those looked like a real effect until the sample size or the causal direction got
checked. Two more, cut for the same reason: eating close to bedtime shows a real recovery
delta (-6.1), but it's almost certainly the late-bedtime story again in a different costume,
not an independent effect. And sleep efficiency, covered above, looks like it should matter
and doesn't. A correlation in your own data about your own body is still just a correlation.

There's also a faster, sharper crash worth separating from all of the above: 22 days where
skin temperature jumped more than half a degree above its own two-week baseline — WHOOP's
early-illness signal — show recovery cratering to 40.3%, against a 58.2% baseline. It's gone
by the next day, back to 56.2%. That's a different failure mode than anything sleep-driven:
acute, not cumulative, and not something a bedtime change fixes.

February is the one month that breaks the pattern above, and it isn't really about sleep or
training at all. One week inside it — February 15 to 21, Mexico City — is the worst week of
the entire year by a wide margin: 18.3% average recovery against a 59.0% baseline for every
other week. The signature is textbook altitude, not just a bad week of sleep: resting heart
rate up 12.8 bpm, HRV down 44 ms, blood oxygen down 2.2 points, all three moving together in
the direction elevation predicts, on top of 1.24 fewer hours of sleep a night from staying out
late in an unfamiliar place. Short sleep alone doesn't explain it — nights under 5 hours
everywhere else in the year average 32.3% recovery, 14 points better than this week managed.
Four of the year's twelve resting-heart-rate-over-60 days happened inside this single week.

<AltitudeCompareChart />

Even against February's own average — already a below-par month — the trip week is worse on
every metric, Day Strain included (WHOOP's 0-21 training-load score, where higher means a
harder day). Worth saying plainly, since the point of the confounds above is not to blur two
different things into one explanation. (Recovery by month is in the appendix.)

## Activity levels

The cardio and soccer goals from [summer 2024](/writing/summer-2024) held up, and then some.
Soccer: 47 sessions, comfortably past the "twice a week" target. Running: 27 sessions, past
"once a week" too. Strength training turned out to be the real backbone of the year though:
114 sessions, about 91 hours, more than anything else. The rest of the year was a lot more
varied than planned: swimming, volleyball, badminton, pickleball, dance, yoga, even one
session each of padel and boxing.

<ActivitiesChart />

<TrainingVolumeChart />

Training load went up over the year while recovery went down — monthly sessions grew from 35
in September 2025 to 80 in August 2026, hours from 25 to 56. Training load grew faster than
sleep could support it.

<StreakChart />

One hard day (63.3% recovery) doesn't cost anything — if anything it's a day I was already
fresh enough to push. Two in a row is still fine (57.6%, basically baseline). The break comes
on day three: ten streaks over the year hit strain 14+ for three or more consecutive days, and
by that third day recovery is 39.2% and REM is down to 73.8 minutes, against a 95.2-minute
baseline. The actionable version isn't "train less," it's "never three hard days back to back
without a real rest day between."
